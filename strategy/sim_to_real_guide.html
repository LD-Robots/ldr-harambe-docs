Part I

Where you actually are.

An honest read on the current training approach, and what it's not telling you.

§ 01 — Assessing the current approach

"Kinda works" is meaningful, but not enough.

You trained policies with URDF + mass randomization and got ones that "kinda work." That's a real achievement — it means the policy is learning robust strategies rather than overfitting to a specific trajectory. But it's also the ceiling of what mass-only randomization can give you, and here's why.

What's working

  • The policy is learning locomotion-compatible control strategies, not memorized trajectories.
  • Mass randomization gives you robustness along one real axis of uncertainty (payloads, assembly tolerance).
  • The core pipeline — URDF ingest, training, ONNX export, deployment — is evidently end-to-end functional.

Why it plateaus

Mass is not the dominant sim-to-real gap. Policies trained only against mass variation become robust to mass and brittle to everything else. When they deploy and "kinda work," what's actually happening is that the policy found strategies that happen to tolerate the unmodeled effects — not because it learned to handle them, but because mass happens to overlap with them stochastically during training. That's fragile.

The failure mode to watch for. Policies that succeed in 7 out of 10 deployments on identical hardware, with no pattern to the failures. That's the signature of an unmodeled dynamic effect: the policy works when the unmodeled term happens to be small, and fails when it isn't. Adding more mass randomization will not fix this — you have to add the right randomization.
§ 02 — The real sim-to-real gap hierarchy

What actually makes the policy fail on real hardware.

For most humanoid tasks, the rank-ordered list of what kills transfer looks like this. Mass uncertainty — the thing you've been randomizing — is near the bottom.

Sim-to-real gap by source
relative impact · typical humanoid task
Longer bars mean bigger real-world effect when unmodeled. This is approximate and task-dependent, but the ordering is remarkably stable across dynamic tasks — actuator dynamics dominate, mass is near the floor.

What to add, in priority order

  • Match the PD layer exactly between sim and real If your Isaac env applies some default PD with Kp = 100 under the hood, and your X6 runs Kp = 3, the policy learned to exploit stiffness that doesn't physically exist. This is a silent failure — no one tells you about the implicit PD in Isaac. Read the actuator config in your training wrapper, and make sure it mirrors the real PD gains.
  • Model actuator dynamics, at minimum a first-order torque lag Real X-series motors have τ_m ≈ 5–15 ms. Default sim actuators have 0. Adding a single low-pass filter between policy output and applied torque closes a huge chunk of the gap, and costs almost nothing in training throughput.
  • Randomize friction around identified values F_c ±30%, F_v ±30%, sampled per episode. You measured these on the bench — randomize around measurements, not guesses.
  • Inject observation and action latency 1–3 loop periods of delay, uniform randomized. A policy trained with zero latency outputs high-frequency corrections that destabilize real hardware on contact.
  • Freeze observation normalization at export If you used PPO with running mean/std, the running stats keep updating during training. Freeze them before ONNX export or your deployed policy sees a different observation distribution than the one it was trained on. This is the single most common silent failure in sim-to-real pipelines.
Domain randomization alone is a blunt tool. Parameter identification plus targeted randomization is a scalpel. You have the identified parameters — use them.
Part II

Isaac, with a baseline.

What one identified joint actually unlocks in simulation — and why the pendulum is the fastest path to the humanoid.

§ 03 — What one identified joint unlocks

Why the pendulum is a feature, not a detour.

You identified J, mgl, F_c, F_v, τ_m on the bench in an hour. With those numbers in hand, a single-joint Isaac environment becomes seven different experiments, each of which teaches you something you'll need on the humanoid. In order of value:

1. Model validation before scaling up

Build the pendulum in Isaac with your identified parameters. Run the same trajectory you run on real hardware. Compare the traces. If they diverge by more than ~10%, your model is wrong — and it's much better to find that out on one joint than on a humanoid with 25 of them.

This is the single highest-leverage thing to do right now. Most humanoid sim-to-real failures trace back to skipping this validation at the single-joint stage.

2. Train a policy that beats hand-tuned PD

A learned torque-control policy on a single pendulum can outperform fixed-gain PD on three axes: faster step response without overshoot, better disturbance rejection, graceful torque saturation handling. Training time: minutes. The value isn't the policy itself — it's that you debug the whole pipeline (reward shaping, observation design, PPO hyperparameters, ONNX export, deployment) at a scale where you can still see what's happening.

3. Swing-up and balance

Classic RL benchmark, fits your rig perfectly. Let the 1 kg mass rotate past the axis and train the policy to bring it from hanging-down to inverted-balanced. Two regimes in one task: energy pumping far from upright, stabilization near it. Great test of policy quality, great photo-op for reports.

4. Adaptive control under payload changes

Train with the 1 kg mass randomized between 0.5 and 2 kg, distance 0.2–0.4 m. Deploy. Swap the weight mid-run. Watch how fast the policy adapts. That's exactly the skill your humanoid needs — arms pick up unknown objects every minute.

5. Latency-budget characterization

Inject varying action latency (0–20 ms) during training. Measure real-world performance vs latency. Find the point where transfer degrades. That number tells you how much latency headroom you have before you scale up.

6. Tune the domain randomization ranges themselves

On one joint you can actually answer: "does ±30% on F_c help or hurt transfer?" Grid-search the randomization bounds and measure transfer quality. The answers generalize — ranges that work for one joint work for others.

7. Prove the ROS 2 deployment pipeline end-to-end

ONNX → ros2_control → EtherCAT → motor. Debug the plumbing on one joint, where a failure just means a bar stops moving, not a robot faceplants. When you scale up, the only new problem is multi-joint coordination — the plumbing is solved.

The pendulum isn't a simpler version of the humanoid. It's the same problem at a scale where you can debug it.
§ 04 — A four-week plan

From tuned bench to policy-transfer-ready.

A concrete sequence for the next month on the single-joint rig. Each week builds on the previous one; by the end, every piece of the humanoid pipeline has been proven at single-joint scale.

Week 1 · Validation
Prove the simulation matches reality.

Build the pendulum URDF in Isaac with identified J, mgl, F_c, F_v, τ_m. Run the same quintic sweep on both real and sim. Overlay traces, compute tracking-error RMSE. Target: < 10%. If it's higher, debug the model before training anything. No policy training this week.

Week 2 · First policy
Train a position-tracking policy; transfer it.

Simplest possible RL task — match a reference trajectory. Train in Isaac with the identified-parameter env, export to ONNX, deploy via ros2_control. The policy doesn't need to beat your PD; it needs to work. What you're actually validating is the training → export → deployment chain.

Week 3 · Harder task
Swing-up + balance — same pipeline, new task.

Now that the plumbing is proven, scale task difficulty. Swing-up forces the policy to learn two different control regimes, and tests whether your actuator model captures saturation correctly. If it works in sim but not real, that's a model gap — usually friction or torque limit.

Week 4 · Robustness tools
Payload randomization + latency study.

Randomize mass and arm length in training; test real-world adaptation when you swap weights. In parallel, sweep action latency during training and measure transfer quality. By end of week 4, you have empirical DR ranges that you know work — instead of the ±20% you guessed at.

Week 5+ · Scale up
Apply the proven pipeline to the humanoid.

Everything you built at pendulum scale — the actuator model, the randomization ranges, the deployment plumbing, the validation protocol — copies over. The new problem is multi-joint coordination. That's still hard, but it's the only remaining hard problem.

Part III

Three simulators, one contract.

Isaac for training, Gazebo for ROS integration, real hardware for final test — each layer catches different bug classes.

§ 05 — Isaac → Gazebo → real

Why three stages, not two.

The intuitive pipeline is train-in-sim, deploy-on-real. In practice, inserting Gazebo between them is what makes the jump reliable. Three layers of decreasing speed and increasing realism, each catching bugs the others can't.

Stage 1
Isaac

Train the policy. Thousands of parallel envs on GPU. Actuator model identified + randomized.

The contract that makes this work

ros2_control defines a stable interface surface — the hardware_interface::SystemInterface class — that both Gazebo and real hardware implement. Your controllers, your ONNX inference node, your launch files, your parameter YAMLs — none of them change between Gazebo and real. Only the plugin underneath does.

That's the contract. Stage 2 and Stage 3 are the same ROS graph with one swap: gz_ros2_control/GazeboSimSystem becomes your_package/RealX6CAN. If you architect for this from day one, deployment is a launch-file parameter change.

The value of ros2_control isn't the controllers — you'll write your own. It's the stable contract between controllers and hardware that makes sim and real interchangeable.
§ 06 — What each layer catches

Different sims, different bug classes.

Skipping any layer means discovering its bugs on real hardware, which is the most expensive place to find them.

Bugs Isaac catches

  • Policy-architecture bugs (reward misspecification, observation normalization, action scaling)
  • Training instability (PPO hyperparameters, entropy collapse, value-function saturation)
  • Gross domain-randomization problems (too narrow, too broad, wrong distribution shape)
  • ONNX export issues (ops that don't serialize, missing metadata)

Bugs Gazebo catches that Isaac doesn't

  • Message-rate mismatches — policy runs at 50 Hz, controller at 1 kHz, state publishes at 500 Hz — does your interpolation work?
  • Observation-assembly bugs — did you compute θ̇ the same way in both sims?
  • Action-scaling bugs — policy outputs ±1 normalized, did you un-normalize before sending torque?
  • Coordinate-frame mismatches — Isaac might have +Z up, URDF says +Y, Gazebo says +Z…
  • DDS middleware latency — QoS settings, network topology if distributed
  • Controller-manager scheduling — does your 1 kHz effort command actually hit the joint at 1 kHz?
  • Launch-file correctness — if it doesn't launch cleanly in Gazebo, it won't on real
  • Interaction with other ROS nodes (logging, visualization, teleop overrides)

Bugs only real hardware catches

  • Electrical noise, EMI on CAN / EtherCAT lines
  • Thermal drift — motor parameters change over a 30-minute run
  • Actuator wear — bearings dry out, brushes wear, friction grows
  • Mechanical issues — backlash, bolt loosening, link flex you didn't model
  • Safety events — e-stops, torque trips, comm timeouts
The honest warning about Gazebo. Default Gazebo actuators are even more idealized than Isaac — pure torque sources with zero lag, perfect sensors, no friction unless you add it. If you skip friction and lag injection in Gazebo, your policy will look perfect there and fail on real. Gazebo catches ROS bugs, not physics bugs, unless you explicitly model actuator imperfections. That's what Part IV is about.
Part IV

Building the Gazebo layer.

A simulated actuator that disagrees with the commanded torque the same way your real motor does.

§ 07 — Why default Gazebo fails

An ideal torque source is not a motor.

Gazebo's default actuator is perfect. Command a torque, the joint experiences that torque, instantly, with zero friction, zero saturation, zero lag. Your policy trains against this and learns strategies that exploit an environment your real motor cannot provide.

Torque response to a 5 N·m step command
ideal vs realistic actuator
The real motor (and a first-order lag model) rises to commanded torque with a ~10 ms time constant. Gazebo's default is the flat rose line — no dynamics. A policy trained against the flat line has no experience of transient behavior and over-commands when deployed.

§ 08 — Architecture

Where does the actuator model live?

One question to answer before any code: at which point in the ROS 2 pipeline do you modify the torque? There are three sensible answers — before the controller, inside the hardware interface, or inside the physics engine itself — and they correspond to Options 2, 3, and 4.

Default (top row) leaves the torque command unchanged. Opt 2 and Opt 3 insert the actuator model at different points. Opt 3 mirrors the structure of your real-hardware interface — swap one plugin for another at deployment.

§ 10 — Option 2

A Python wrapper node before the controller.

The actuator model lives in its own ROS 2 node that sits between your policy and the effort controller. Policy publishes raw torque; wrapper consumes it, applies the model, republishes to the controller. Full implementation below — paste into actuator_wrapper.py, adjust topic names, launch alongside everything else.

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""
Actuator-model wrapper for Gazebo sim-to-real validation.

Subscribes:  /policy_effort_raw     (Float64MultiArray)
             /joint_states          (sensor_msgs/JointState)
Publishes:   /effort_controllers/commands  (Float64MultiArray)

Applies per joint:  saturation → first-order torque lag → friction.
Exposes params via ros2 param set for domain randomization.
"""

import numpy as np
import rclpy
from rclpy.node import Node
from sensor_msgs.msg import JointState
from std_msgs.msg import Float64MultiArray


class ActuatorWrapper(Node):

    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__('actuator_wrapper')

        # ── identified motor params — from the ID procedure ─
        self.declare_parameter('Fc',      0.15)    # Coulomb, N·m
        self.declare_parameter('Fv',      0.05)    # viscous, N·m·s/rad
        self.declare_parameter('tau_m',   0.010)   # motor lag, s
        self.declare_parameter('tau_max', 12.0)    # hard limit, N·m
        self.declare_parameter('rate_hz', 1000.0)
        self.declare_parameter('joint_name', 'pendulum_joint')

        # ── state ───────────────────────────────────────
        self.tau_applied = 0.0      # filtered torque (lag state)
        self.tau_cmd     = 0.0      # latest raw command from policy
        self.velocity    = 0.0      # latest measured joint velocity

        rate = self.get_parameter('rate_hz').value
        self.dt = 1.0 / rate

        # ── ROS I/O ─────────────────────────────────────
        self.create_subscription(
            Float64MultiArray, '/policy_effort_raw',
            self.on_policy_cmd, 10)
        self.create_subscription(
            JointState, '/joint_states',
            self.on_joint_state, 10)
        self.pub = self.create_publisher(
            Float64MultiArray, '/effort_controllers/commands', 10)

        self.create_timer(self.dt, self.tick)

    def on_policy_cmd(self, msg):
        if len(msg.data) > 0:
            self.tau_cmd = float(msg.data[0])

    def on_joint_state(self, msg):
        name = self.get_parameter('joint_name').value
        if name in msg.name:
            i = msg.name.index(name)
            self.velocity = msg.velocity[i]

    def tick(self):
        Fc      = self.get_parameter('Fc').value
        Fv      = self.get_parameter('Fv').value
        tau_m   = self.get_parameter('tau_m').value
        tau_max = self.get_parameter('tau_max').value

        # 1. saturate raw policy command
        tau_c = np.clip(self.tau_cmd, -tau_max, tau_max)

        # 2. first-order torque lag
        alpha = self.dt / max(tau_m, 1e-6)
        self.tau_applied += (tau_c - self.tau_applied) * alpha

        # 3. friction (opposes motion; tanh replaces sign for smoothness)
        tau_fric = Fc * np.tanh(self.velocity / 0.01) + Fv * self.velocity

        # 4. publish net torque
        out = Float64MultiArray()
        out.data = [float(self.tau_applied - tau_fric)]
        self.pub.publish(out)


def main():
    rclpy.init()
    rclpy.spin(ActuatorWrapper())

Why this works

  • Policy publishes to /policy_effort_raw; wrapper corrupts it with realistic effects and forwards to the controller.
  • All params are ROS 2 parameters — randomize at training time via ros2 param set between episodes.
  • Same node runs during training and Gazebo validation; remove it for real hardware (the motor has its own lag and friction).
Timing gotcha. The wrapper runs on its own timer, independent of the controller-manager tick. In heavy sim you can miss cycles. If you see sim latency issues, move to Option 3 — model runs inside the write() callback and executes every control tick.
§ 11 — Option 3

A custom hardware_interface plugin.

The architecturally correct answer. A SystemInterface plugin whose write() method applies the actuator model before forwarding to the Gazebo joint API. Your real-hardware X6 CAN interface becomes a sibling class with the same signature.

// include/simulated_x6/simulated_x6.hpp
#pragma once
#include <hardware_interface/system_interface.hpp>

namespace simulated_x6 {

class SimulatedX6 : public hardware_interface::SystemInterface {
public:
  CallbackReturn on_init(const hardware_interface::HardwareInfo &) override;
  CallbackReturn on_activate  (const rclcpp_lifecycle::State &) override;
  CallbackReturn on_deactivate(const rclcpp_lifecycle::State &) override;

  std::vector<hardware_interface::StateInterface>   export_state_interfaces()   override;
  std::vector<hardware_interface::CommandInterface> export_command_interfaces() override;

  hardware_interface::return_type read (const rclcpp::Time &, const rclcpp::Duration &) override;
  hardware_interface::return_type write(const rclcpp::Time &, const rclcpp::Duration &) override;

private:
  double Fc_{0.15}, Fv_{0.05}, tau_m_{0.010}, tau_max_{12.0};
  std::vector<double> position_, velocity_, effort_;
  std::vector<double> commanded_effort_, tau_applied_;
};

}  // namespace

// src/simulated_x6.cpp — just the write() method
hardware_interface::return_type SimulatedX6::write(
    const rclcpp::Time &, const rclcpp::Duration & period)
{
  const double dt = period.seconds();

  for (size_t i = 0; i < commanded_effort_.size(); ++i) {
    // 1. saturate
    double tau_c = std::clamp(commanded_effort_[i], -tau_max_, tau_max_);

    // 2. first-order torque lag
    const double alpha = dt / std::max(tau_m_, 1e-6);
    tau_applied_[i] += (tau_c - tau_applied_[i]) * alpha;

    // 3. friction (tanh replaces sign to avoid chatter)
    const double v = velocity_[i];
    const double tau_f = Fc_ * std::tanh(v / 0.01) + Fv_ * v;

    // 4. net torque to forward
    effort_[i] = tau_applied_[i] - tau_f;
  }
  return hardware_interface::return_type::OK;
}

Why this is the long-term answer

  • Model runs inside write(), guaranteed every control tick.
  • Identical interface surface to your real-hardware plugin (RealX6CAN). Deployment is a URDF edit.
  • Parameters settable at runtime via services — domain randomization just works.
  • Same plugin architecture wraps MuJoCo, Isaac, Gazebo, or real hardware. Swap transport, keep model.

§ 13 — Domain randomization in simulation

§ 14 — Validating sim matches real

The one test before trusting the pipeline.

Before training any policy against the sim, verify the sim reproduces real-world behavior. Run the same trajectory on real hardware and in Gazebo with your model active. Overlay, compute RMSE. If they diverge by more than ~10%, fix the model — not the policy.

  • Pick a test trajectory Smooth quintic sweep from 0 to 0.5 rad in 1 second, return to 0 in another 1 second. Wide enough to excite friction, fast enough to excite lag.
  • Record real trace Run the trajectory on the X6 with your PD + feedforward. Log /joint_states at 1 kHz to a ros2 bag.
  • Record sim trace Same trajectory, same PD gains, same feedforward, same launch file — just gz_ros2_control + actuator wrapper/plugin instead of the hardware driver.
  • Overlay and compute RMSE < 5% — excellent. 5–10% — acceptable. > 10% — debug.
  • Diagnose divergence modes Sim leads real by a constant offset → you missed the motor lag. Sim tracks tighter at low speed → missed Coulomb friction. Sim tracks tighter at high speed → F_v wrong. Sim has zero steady-state error, real has bias → gravity feedforward isn't identical between the two.

§ 15 — End-to-end roadmap

From one motor, a humanoid.

Every piece of the pipeline at the single-joint stage maps directly onto a piece of the humanoid pipeline. Build it once, carefully, and scale up with confidence.

What "pipeline proven" means

  • You can train a policy in Isaac with identified parameters.
  • You can validate it in Gazebo via gz_ros2_control with the actuator model active.
  • You can deploy the same ONNX to real hardware by changing one URDF parameter.
  • You can reproduce real-hardware behavior in sim to within 10% RMSE on a test trajectory.
  • You can re-identify a joint's parameters in under an hour and have them flow into training.

When all five of those are true on the pendulum, you're ready to scale. The humanoid adds joints, contacts, balance — real technical complexity — but it doesn't add pipeline complexity. That's already solved.

Don't start with the humanoid. Start with the joint you already have. Identify its parameters, build the simulated actuator, get a policy transferring through all three stages, measure the sim-vs-real gap empirically. Every hour spent on the pendulum is an hour you don't spend debugging the humanoid.

Once a single joint survives the full Isaac → Gazebo → real loop with measured transfer quality, multiplying it by 25 joints is engineering — not research.
