§ 01 — Plant model

The equation of motion, before anything else.

Every control decision you make traces back to one differential equation. For a single revolute joint driving a rigid body, Newton's second law in rotational form is:

Eq. 1 — joint dynamics $$ J\,\ddot\theta \;+\; b\,\dot\theta \;+\; \tau_f(\dot\theta) \;+\; mgl\sin\theta \;=\; \tau $$
J
total rotational inertia reflected at the joint axis (kg·m²)
b
viscous friction coefficient (N·m·s/rad)
τf
non-linear friction — dry Coulomb, stiction, etc. (N·m)
mgl
gravity torque amplitude; m = mass, l = distance from axis to COM (N·m)
θ
joint angle, measured from the position where gravity torque is zero (rad)
τ
applied motor torque — this is what you command (N·m)

Everything downstream — PD gains, feedforward terms, what your ONNX policy expects — is just a strategy for picking τ given the right-hand side you know and the disturbances you don't.

Your setup is this equation. The 70 cm bar and 1 kg mass define J and mgl; the X6's internal impedance loop computes τ from Kp, Kd, and τff. Get the right-hand side right and the policy transfers.
§ 02 — Estimating J

You can't tune what you haven't measured.

Before touching gains, compute J. Every gain formula depends on it, and a wrong J by 2× will silently kill your controller.

Analytical estimate — your rig

For a thin uniform bar rotating about its center, plus a point mass offset along it:

Eq. 2 — rigid-body inertia $$ J \;=\; \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{12}\, m_{\text{bar}}\, L^2}_{\text{bar about center}} \;+\; \underbrace{m_w\, r^2}_{\text{point mass offset}} \;+\; J_{\text{rotor}} $$

Plugging in your rig (assume a ~300 g aluminum bar, 1 kg weight at 30 cm, rotor inertia small):

$$ J \;\approx\; \tfrac{1}{12}(0.30)(0.70)^2 \;+\; (1.0)(0.30)^2 \;=\; 0.01225 + 0.0900 \;\approx\; \mathbf{0.102\ \text{kg·m}^2} $$

The offset mass dominates by a factor of ~7. That's useful intuition: when you move the 1 kg further out, inertia grows quadratically, but gravity torque only grows linearly. This is why balancing problems get harder faster than they look.

Experimental methods — pick one, sanity-check with another

  • Pendulum period Let it swing freely under gravity as a physical pendulum. Measure the period T (average over 10+ cycles). Then: $$ T = 2\pi\sqrt{\tfrac{J}{mgl}} \;\Longrightarrow\; J = \tfrac{mgl\,T^2}{4\pi^2} $$ Cheap, no torque sensor needed, gives you mgl and J together.
  • Torque-step response Command a torque step τ₀ from rest at the zero-gravity angle (horizontal axis configuration if possible, or subtract gravity). Fit θ̈ ≈ τ₀/J from the first ~20 ms before friction dominates.
  • Sinusoidal excitation + fit Sweep a chirp torque τ(t) = A sin(ω t) with ω ramping. Fit the transfer function θ(s)/τ(s) = 1/(Js² + bs + k). The asymptotic −40 dB/dec slope gives you J.

I'd recommend the pendulum method first — it's 10 minutes of work and gives you a number you can trust to ~5%. Then torque step to cross-check.

§ 03 — PD gains from first principles

Why Kp, Kd aren't random knobs.

When you apply a PD controller $\tau = K_p(\theta_d - \theta) + K_d(\dot\theta_d - \dot\theta)$ to a pure inertia, the closed loop becomes a linear second-order system. That's the whole game: you're shaping a mass-spring-damper where you get to pick the spring and the damper.

Eq. 3 — closed-loop dynamics $$ J\,\ddot\theta \;+\; K_d\,\dot\theta \;+\; K_p\,\theta \;=\; K_p\,\theta_d $$

Compare this to the canonical second-order form:

$$ \ddot\theta \;+\; 2\zeta\omega_n\,\dot\theta \;+\; \omega_n^2\,\theta \;=\; \omega_n^2\,\theta_d $$

and two identities fall out that you will use for the rest of your career:

Eq. 4 — the PD design equations $$ \boxed{\; K_p = J\,\omega_n^2 \;} \qquad\qquad \boxed{\; K_d = 2\,\zeta\,\sqrt{K_p\,J} = 2\,\zeta\,\omega_n\,J \;} $$
ωn
natural frequency = how fast the loop responds (rad/s). Higher = snappier, but limited by noise, sample rate, and actuator bandwidth.
ζ
damping ratio = how much it rings. 0.7 is the tracking sweet spot, 1.0 is critical (no overshoot), 0.3 is ringy, >1.5 is sluggish.
  • Pick ωn first based on what the physical system can actually do. Upper bound: ωn ≤ (1/5 to 1/10) of your control loop rate in rad/s. At 1 kHz → ωn ≤ 100–200 rad/s.
  • Start with ζ = 0.7. It's the damping ratio that minimizes settling time without overshoot problems. Move toward 1.0 if you can't tolerate any overshoot (e.g., against a hard stop).
  • Then set Kp, Kd from the formulas. Don't eyeball. You'll be within 20% of optimal on the first try.
  • Verify closed-loop bandwidth empirically with a chirp reference. If your measured bandwidth is much lower than ωn, you're torque-saturating or hitting an unmodeled actuator lag.

§ 05 — Your rig, worked out

From J and a bandwidth goal to actual gain numbers.

Let's do the arithmetic for your stand. You measured gravity feedforward Kg ≈ 3.0 N·m, which implies mgl ≈ 2.94 N·m — consistent with 1 kg × 9.81 × 0.30 = 2.943 N·m. That checks. Now the PD part.

Target bandwidth ωn

Damping ζ
0.70

Standard tracking target. No overshoot? Go 1.0.

Step 1 — compute Kp from ωn $$ K_p = J\omega_n^2 = 0.102 \times 15^2 = 0.102 \times 225 = \mathbf{22.95\ \text{N·m/rad}} $$
Step 2 — compute Kd from ζ $$ K_d = 2\zeta\sqrt{K_p J} = 2(0.7)\sqrt{22.95 \times 0.102} = 1.4 \times 1.531 \approx \mathbf{2.14\ \text{N·m·s/rad}} $$
Sanity check against what you have. Your current Kp = 3.0, Kd = 0.66 gives ωn = √(3.0/0.102) ≈ 5.4 rad/s and ζ = 0.66 / (2√(3.0 × 0.102)) ≈ 0.59. That's ~3× softer than the target above but still stable with good damping — which is exactly what you're reporting. It also explains why it tracks a 0.1 s trajectory "beautifully": gravity feedforward is doing most of the work, the PD is just cleaning up. To actually stress-test the PD, you want to remove the feedforward temporarily or introduce a disturbance step.

So: your next experiment should be stepping Kp toward ~20 and Kd toward ~2, keeping ζ ≈ 0.7 as you go. If it starts ringing or chattering before you get there, the limit is motor bandwidth, sensor noise, or loop rate — not the formulas.

§ 06 — Feedforward I

Gravity compensation — your zero-error anchor.

A PD loop alone always has a small steady-state error under gravity, because producing a non-zero torque at zero velocity requires a non-zero position error. Feedforward eliminates that error by predicting the torque and handing it to the motor before the PD has to react.

Eq. 5 — gravity feedforward $$ \tau_{\text{grav}}(\theta) \;=\; m\,g\,l\,\sin\theta $$

Your current implementation uses K_g = mgl as a constant — which is exactly right if θ is measured from horizontal and the bar only moves through small angles. For the humanoid, where joints swing through 90° or more, you must include the sin(θ):

$$ \tau_{\text{grav}} = K_g \sin(\theta) \qquad\text{with}\qquad K_g = mgl $$
Gravity torque vs joint angle
your rig · Kg = 2.943 N·m
θ = 0 is the position where the bar is horizontal (maximum gravity torque). At θ = 90° the bar points straight up or down — gravity torque is zero, but you're at an unstable or stable equilibrium depending on sign.

Multi-link case — the general form

For a full humanoid, gravity torque on joint i depends on every link distal to it. In terms of the robot's potential energy U(q):

$$ \boldsymbol{\tau}_g(\mathbf{q}) \;=\; \frac{\partial U(\mathbf{q})}{\partial \mathbf{q}} $$

You won't compute this by hand; Pinocchio, RBDL, and drake::multibody all export this as computeGeneralizedGravity(q). Run it at your controller rate and feed it directly to your effort interface. Gravity compensation is the single highest-leverage feedforward term — do it first.

§ 07 — Feedforward II

Inertia feedforward — so PD doesn't have to push the mass around.

If you command a fast trajectory, the PD controller has to generate a lag-then-catch-up torque profile. You can hand the acceleration term directly to the motor and leave PD to correct disturbances, not accelerate inertia.

Eq. 6 — full inverse-dynamics feedforward, single joint $$ \tau_{\text{ff}} \;=\; J\,\ddot\theta_d \;+\; b\,\dot\theta_d \;+\; \tau_f(\dot\theta_d) \;+\; mgl\sin\theta_d $$

For a 0.1 s trajectory moving 0.5 rad with a quintic profile, peak acceleration is roughly:

$$ \ddot\theta_{\text{peak}} \approx \frac{10}{\sqrt{3}} \cdot \frac{\Delta\theta}{T^2} = \frac{10}{\sqrt{3}} \cdot \frac{0.5}{0.01} \approx 289\ \text{rad/s}^2 $$
$$ \tau_{\text{inertia,peak}} = J \cdot \ddot\theta_{\text{peak}} = 0.102 \times 289 \approx \mathbf{29.5\ \text{N·m}} $$

That's an order of magnitude larger than the gravity feedforward. Without inertia FF, the entire 29.5 N·m has to come out of the PD loop — which means either huge tracking error, or huge Kp (which amplifies noise). With inertia FF, the PD sees a disturbance rejection problem, not a motion generation problem. This is the single biggest win for fast trajectories.

  • Cubic splines: differentiate twice analytically. Cheap, widely supported, slight acceleration discontinuities at knots.
  • Quintic (5th-order) polynomials: smooth acceleration, zero jerk at boundaries. Use this for test-stand trajectories.
  • Trapezoidal velocity profile: constant θ̈ in three phases. Simple but has discontinuous jerk.
  • Ruckig / Reflexxes / TOTG: time-optimal jerk-limited online generators. This is what your humanoid should use in production.
A good mental model: PD = disturbance rejection bandwidth. Feedforward = trajectory tracking. They solve different problems with the same motor.
§ 08 — Feedforward III

Friction — the sneaky term that eats your repeatability.

Friction is the difference between simulation and reality. Every policy trained without it will hit the real motor, lose 10–30% of its low-speed torque, and fail in ways that look like "controller noise" but are actually unmodeled Coulomb friction.

A practical model

Eq. 7 — Coulomb + viscous friction $$ \tau_f(\dot\theta) \;=\; F_c \cdot \operatorname{sign}(\dot\theta) \;+\; F_v \cdot \dot\theta $$
Fc
Coulomb (dry) friction — constant torque opposing motion regardless of speed (N·m)
Fv
viscous friction coefficient — proportional to velocity (N·m·s/rad)

More elaborate models (Stribeck, LuGre) capture stiction and the velocity-weakening dip, but Coulomb + viscous covers 90% of what a humanoid policy needs.

Steady-state torque vs velocity
friction identification plot
The y-intercept (extrapolated from small |θ̇|) is Fc. The slope is Fv. Near zero velocity, stiction breaks the clean line — don't fit there. Assumed Fc = 0.15 N·m, Fv = 0.05 N·m·s/rad for this illustration.

How to identify Fc and Fv

  • Command constant velocities in both directions Pick ~8 velocities between −2 and +2 rad/s, avoiding 0. Hold each long enough to reach steady state (~1 second).
  • At steady state, τ = τgrav(θ) + τf(θ̇) Subtract the gravity term analytically. What remains is pure friction.
  • Plot the residual vs velocity and fit a line Separately for each direction. The two y-intercepts give you ±Fc, the slopes give you Fv. Asymmetry is common — geartrain friction is rarely symmetric.
  • Validate Inject −τf(θ̇d) as feedforward. Repeat a slow trajectory with and without it. Tracking error should drop dramatically at low speeds where friction dominates.
sign(θ̇) discontinuity. Using sign() directly causes chattering at zero crossings. Replace with tanh(θ̇/ε) where ε ≈ 0.01 rad/s, or gate the Coulomb term when |θ̇| falls below a threshold.
§ 09 — Motor characterization protocol

A one-day procedure to know your actuator cold.

Do this once per joint type. The numbers you produce here are what you'll feed into your simulator's domain randomization. Everything downstream — policy training, sim-to-real — depends on these being accurate.

  • Zero position & direction Define θ = 0 and the positive direction of rotation. Write it down. Every subsequent measurement references this.
  • Gravity term mgl Hang the arm at different angles. Command zero velocity, log the torque the motor actually outputs. Plot τ vs θ, fit a sine. Amplitude is mgl, phase tells you where horizontal is.
  • Inertia J Method A — pendulum: let it swing freely, measure period, apply $J = mglT^2/(4\pi^2)$. Method B — torque step: at horizontal (where gravity = 0), command a known torque step. Fit $\ddot\theta$ over the first 10–20 ms → $J = \tau/\ddot\theta$. Numbers from both methods should agree within 10%.
  • Viscous friction Fv Command constant velocities, record steady-state torque after subtracting gravity. Slope of τresidual vs θ̇ is Fv.
  • Coulomb friction Fc Same data, but the y-intercept (extrapolating away from zero). Often asymmetric — record both Fc⁺ and Fc⁻.
  • Stiction Fs Slowly ramp torque at zero velocity. The torque where motion first begins is Fs. Typically 1.5–2× Fc.
  • Torque constant Kt Command known currents (if the actuator exposes current mode) at a fixed, gravity-known angle. Balance against gravity: Kt = τgrav / i. Or use the manufacturer spec as a starting point and calibrate ±10%.
  • Torque bandwidth / motor lag τm Command a step in torque. Measure time to 63% of the commanded value. That's your first-order motor time constant τm, typically 5–15 ms for X-series actuators. Put this in the simulator.
  • Sensor latency & loop jitter Command a torque impulse. Log timestamps of (a) command sent, (b) first encoder movement observed. The delay is your total actuation latency. Measure jitter over 10 000 samples — if standard deviation > 0.1 × loop period, fix your scheduling before continuing.
  • Backlash Command small oscillations around zero position, plot commanded vs measured angle. The dead zone is your backlash. For a harmonic drive or cycloidal it's tiny; for a planetary it can be degrees.
Save every raw log. You will re-identify as actuators wear, bearings dry out, and thermal drift changes resistance. Week-to-week drift of 5–15% in Fc is normal. Automate this whole procedure — ~20 minutes if scripted.
§ 10 — Example: Python controller

A minimal PD + full feedforward loop.

A reference implementation you can run at 1 kHz with a ros2 control hardware interface underneath, or inside a MuJoCo sim wrapper, or as a CAN callback. The math is the same.

import numpy as np
from dataclasses import dataclass

@dataclass
class JointModel:
    # Identified physical parameters — from §09
    J:  float          # inertia, kg·m²
    mgl: float         # gravity torque amplitude, N·m
    Fc: float          # Coulomb friction, N·m
    Fv: float          # viscous friction, N·m·s/rad
    tau_limit: float   # hardware torque limit, N·m

@dataclass
class PDGains:
    Kp: float
    Kd: float

    @classmethod
    def from_bandwidth(cls, J: float, wn: float, zeta: float = 0.7):
        """Compute Kp, Kd from physical system + desired closed-loop shape."""
        Kp = J * wn**2
        Kd = 2 * zeta * np.sqrt(Kp * J)
        return cls(Kp=Kp, Kd=Kd)


def control_step(
    model: JointModel,
    gains: PDGains,
    theta: float, theta_dot: float,                   # measured state
    theta_d: float, theta_d_dot: float, theta_d_ddot: float,  # reference
    eps: float = 0.01,                              # sign() softener
) -> float:
    """One control cycle. Call at a fixed rate (e.g. 1 kHz)."""

    # ── Feedback: PD on error ─────────────────────────
    e     = theta_d     - theta
    e_dot = theta_d_dot - theta_dot
    tau_fb = gains.Kp * e + gains.Kd * e_dot

    # ── Feedforward: inverse dynamics of the joint ────
    tau_ff_inertia = model.J   * theta_d_ddot
    tau_ff_gravity = model.mgl * np.sin(theta_d)           # sine, not constant!
    tau_ff_viscous = model.Fv  * theta_d_dot
    tau_ff_coulomb = model.Fc  * np.tanh(theta_d_dot / eps) # smooth sign()

    tau_ff = (tau_ff_inertia + tau_ff_gravity
              + tau_ff_viscous + tau_ff_coulomb)

    # ── Combine & saturate ────────────────────────────
    tau_cmd = tau_fb + tau_ff
    tau_cmd = np.clip(tau_cmd, -model.tau_limit, model.tau_limit)

    return tau_cmd


# ── Usage, your rig ──────────────────────────────────
joint = JointModel(J=0.102, mgl=2.943,
                   Fc=0.15, Fv=0.05, tau_limit=12.0)

gains = PDGains.from_bandwidth(J=joint.J, wn=15.0, zeta=0.7)
# → Kp ≈ 22.95  Kd ≈ 2.14

tau = control_step(joint, gains,
                   theta=0.12, theta_dot=0.3,
                   theta_d=0.15, theta_d_dot=0.5, theta_d_ddot=2.0)

Three habits worth enforcing in this loop:

  • Keep physical parameters in a plain dataclass, never sprinkled through the code. When you switch joints or rebuild the simulator, you update one place.
  • Never compute gains inline — always through from_bandwidth() or equivalent. Makes it trivial to sweep ωn and ζ without re-deriving Kp/Kd.
  • Log everything: measured state, reference, each feedforward component, commanded torque, saturated torque, actual current. You cannot debug what you don't log.
§ 11 — ROS 2 effort tuning

Bringing it into ros2_control.

Your test stand eventually runs under ros2_control with an effort interface. The control math above goes inside a controller plugin; what ros2_control gives you is scheduling, hardware abstraction, and a logging surface.

Controller choice

For single-joint bring-up and identification: effort_controllers/JointGroupEffortController. You command raw torques from a higher-level node — maximum flexibility, minimum magic.

For trajectory tracking with built-in PD + feedforward: joint_trajectory_controller with command_interfaces: [effort]. You publish a JointTrajectory; the controller interpolates and applies gains per joint.

Gain mapping in the YAML

joint_trajectory_controller:
  ros__parameters:
    joints: [shoulder_pitch]
    command_interfaces: [effort]
    state_interfaces:   [position, velocity]
    gains:
      shoulder_pitch:
        p: 22.95      # Kp from §05
        d: 2.14       # Kd from §05
        i: 0.0        # leave integral at 0 until drift observed
        ff_velocity_scale:     0.05  # Fv
        ff_acceleration_scale: 0.102 # J

What to measure once it's running

  • Loop rate jitter: log period from update() callbacks. Standard deviation should be < 5% of the nominal period.
  • Command vs measured: publish both at 1 kHz, compute tracking error in post. Plot the error spectrum — peaks tell you resonances.
  • Closed-loop bandwidth: send a chirp reference from 0.1 to 30 Hz, record measured angle, compute Bode magnitude. The frequency where it drops 3 dB is your real bandwidth. That number is what you should match in simulation, not your ωn design target.
The value of ros2_control isn't the controllers — you'll write your own. It's the contract: a hardware interface you can replace with a sim plugin, exposing the exact same state/command surface. That's the machinery that makes sim-to-real tractable.

Filtered joint-state broadcaster

The X6's PDO carries raw position (0x6064), raw velocity (0x606C) and raw effort (0x6077). Its on-drive "Filtered Velocity" (0x200D) and "Filtered Torque" (0x200F) objects are not mapped by any TxPDO on the current firmware — so the pendulum bench reproduces them host-side via pendulum_pd_control/FilteredJointStateBroadcaster, a first-order IIR low-pass running every control cycle (full 1 kHz) and republishing on /filtered_joint_states.

filtered_joint_state_broadcaster:
  ros__parameters:
    type: pendulum_pd_control/FilteredJointStateBroadcaster
    joint: pendulum_joint
    velocity_cutoff_hz: 30.0      # <= 0 disables filtering (raw passthrough)
    torque_cutoff_hz:   30.0
    publish_rate:       1000.0    # Hz — no decimation
    topic: filtered_joint_states  # /filtered_joint_states

Both cutoffs are live-tunable via ros2 param set; setting either to <= 0 turns it back into a passthrough. The broadcaster is only spawned on the real-hardware launch — the Gazebo URDF does not expose the effort state interface and the sim path never loads it.

Effort clamping moved to pendulum_safety

Per-controller clamp_symmetric + tau_limit are gone. Effort, position and velocity caps are now applied by the realtime-safe limiter library from pendulum_safety in every controller update(); breach detection and the latched e-stop live in pendulum_safety_supervisor. The supervisor publishes two latched std_msgs topics that the controllers read with lock-free atomics:

  • /pendulum/safety/estop_state (Int8) — 0 clear / 1 e-stop+FREE / 2 e-stop+HOLD.
  • /pendulum/safety/kp_scale (Float64) — thermal Kp multiplier, [kp_scale_floor, 1.0].

Limit numbers — including the pendulum's velocity_limit = 3.0 rad/s, effort_limit = 20 N·m, slew_rate_limit = 2.5 rad/s — live in a single safety_limits.yaml. See the safety supervisor field note for the full contract, per-trigger FREE/HOLD policy, and integration shape per controller.

§ 12 — Sim-to-real for ONNX

Where most pipelines silently fail.

An ONNX policy is just a function: observation in, action out. "Action" on a humanoid is almost always a set of joint targets or joint torques. The sim-to-real gap is the distance between what the simulator said those actions would do and what the motor actually does. Close that gap in these places:

1. PD gains must match exactly between sim and real

If your policy was trained in Isaac Gym / MuJoCo with Kp = 40, Kd = 2, you must deploy with Kp = 40, Kd = 2. A common failure is training with Isaac's default gains (often enormous, like Kp = 200) and deploying on real hardware that can't physically produce that stiffness. The policy learned to exploit infinite torque and falls apart immediately.

Either lower sim gains to what your hardware supports, or pick realistic gains first and train to those.

2. Actuator dynamics in sim

Default rigid-body simulators model the motor as an ideal torque source. Real motors have:

  • First-order torque lag: τactual(s) = τcmd(s) / (1 + sτm). Add this as a filter between policy output and applied torque. τm ≈ 5–15 ms for X-series.
  • Torque limits: clip to the real peak and continuous limits separately (thermal).
  • Friction: Coulomb + viscous from §08. Huge source of sim-to-real gap when omitted.
  • Backlash: dead-zone between commanded and actual position for bidirectional motion.

3. Domain randomization around real parameters

This is where §09 pays off. You identified Fc, Fv, J, τm from your real hardware. In training:

ParameterRandomizationWhy
J±20% uniformmass estimation error, payloads, wear
Fc, Fv±30% uniformhigh variability over temperature & lifetime
Kp, Kd±10% uniformcontroller timing jitter, gain quantization
mgl±5% uniformlink mass uncertainty
τm (motor lag)5–20 ms uniformtemperature dependence of current loop
Observation latency1–3 loop periodscommunication delays on CAN/EtherCAT
Encoder noise~1 count stdquantization and electrical noise

4. Latency is not optional

Every real control pipeline has delay: encoder read → policy inference → torque command → motor apply. On a well-tuned humanoid this is 2–5 ms. Put it in the simulator. A policy trained with zero latency will output high-frequency corrections that destabilize the real robot.

5. Observation normalization must freeze at deployment

If you used running statistics during PPO training, freeze them before exporting to ONNX. Otherwise your robot sees a different observation distribution than the one the policy was trained on, and performance degrades silently.

The sim-to-real checklist. Before you ever deploy a new policy, verify: (1) identical PD gains in sim and real, (2) actuator lag model present, (3) friction from ID fed into sim, (4) latency injected, (5) domain randomization bracketing real-world values, (6) observation normalization frozen, (7) action space in the same units and scale. Skip any one and you'll spend a week debugging what looks like a learning bug but is a modeling bug.
§ 13 — Automated tuning sweep

A script you'll run on every joint of the humanoid.

You don't want to hand-tune 25+ joints. Scripting the sweep means you characterize a new joint in 20 minutes, not a day. Here's the skeleton — the key insight is that the cost function is tracking RMSE under a step disturbance, not a step reference.

import numpy as np, itertools, json, time

def sweep_gains(hw, joint_name, J_est,
                wn_grid=(10, 15, 20, 30, 45),
                zeta_grid=(0.5, 0.7, 1.0),
                test_duration=2.0):
    """Grid-search (wn, zeta); return table of (rmse, overshoot, tau_peak)."""
    results = []
    for wn, zeta in itertools.product(wn_grid, zeta_grid):
        Kp = J_est * wn**2
        Kd = 2 * zeta * np.sqrt(Kp * J_est)

        hw.set_gains(joint_name, Kp=Kp, Kd=Kd)
        hw.move_to(joint_name, 0.0);  time.sleep(0.5)

        # Step reference: 0 → 0.3 rad
        t, theta, tau = hw.log_step(joint_name,
                                    target=0.3, duration=test_duration)

        err  = 0.3 - np.array(theta)
        rmse = np.sqrt(np.mean(err**2))
        peak = np.max(np.array(theta))
        os_pct = 100 * (peak - 0.3) / 0.3 if peak > 0.3 else 0.0

        results.append({
            "wn": wn, "zeta": zeta,
            "Kp": Kp,  "Kd": Kd,
            "rmse": rmse,
            "overshoot_pct": os_pct,
            "tau_peak": np.max(np.abs(tau)),
        })
        print(f"wn={wn:5.1f}  ζ={zeta:.2f}  rmse={rmse:.4f}  os={os_pct:5.1f}%")

    return results


# ── Post-processing: pick the best gain set ─────────
def pick_best(results, max_overshoot=5.0, max_tau=10.0):
    feasible = [r for r in results
                if r["overshoot_pct"] <= max_overshoot
                   and r["tau_peak"] <= max_tau]
    return min(feasible, key=lambda r: r["rmse"])

Run this once per joint. Save the winning (Kp, Kd, wn, zeta) to a YAML that both the real controller and the simulator load. Now sim and real start from the same gains by construction.

The real upgrade later is Bayesian optimization instead of a grid — fewer trials, better coverage. But a 5×3 grid gets you 90% of the way in 15 joint seconds × 15 configs = 4 minutes per joint.

§ 14 — Per-joint humanoid calibration

From bench gains to a real arm that floats.

The bench-rig math (§01–§09) gives you the form of the answer; the humanoid is where you discover that the URDF link masses are wrong, three of the right-arm motor mounts are mirrored, and one of the wrists is delicate enough that a stale torque step broke it last time. The first version of the gravity-comp node assumed kg = 1 everywhere and the arms drifted to their limits the moment it came up. The humanoid_pd_tuning ROS 2 package is the calibration runtime that closes that loop — same node runs in Gazebo first, then on real hardware, then becomes the production gravcomp/PD controller.

The bench pendulum is a separate identification rig inherited from an earlier project; its J, m·g·l, Fc, Fv have to be re-measured against this exact actuator and bar before its identified-parameter cards (§09 quick reference) apply here. Treat any number imported from there as provisional until verified.

Same node, two lives. During calibration you flip joints into TUNE mode one at a time and dial kg by hand. After the campaign, that same node — with mode = PD and kg ≈ 1 across the board — is what runs in production.

The mode machine

Each joint independently lives in one of three modes. The control law is computed in software inside the tuning node; the EtherCAT drives are booted into CST (mode 10) and never switched at runtime — that path was disabled after the wrist-yaw incident.

Eq. 14a — per-joint torque law $$ \tau_i \;=\; \begin{cases} 0 & \text{mode} = \text{FREE} \\[2pt] s_i\,k_g^{(i)}\,\tau_g^{(i)}(q) & \text{mode} = \text{TUNE} \\[2pt] s_i\,k_g^{(i)}\,\tau_g^{(i)}(q) + k_p^{(i)}(q^d_i - q_i) - k_d^{(i)}\dot q_i & \text{mode} = \text{PD} \end{cases} $$
τg(i)(q)
gravity torque at joint i, computed by PyKDL ChainDynParam.JntToGravity over the full chain — already accounts for the pose of every upstream joint.
kg(i)
per-joint gravity scalar — the thing you're discovering. URDF correct ⇒ kg = 1.
si
±1 motor-mounting flip. On the right arm three joints (shoulder_roll, shoulder_yaw, wrist_yaw) are mirrored; comp_sign = −1.
qdi
PD setpoint. Captured from current position with one click before flipping a joint into PD mode.

FREE means the joint receives zero effort and falls under gravity — you (or a jig) physically support it. TUNE is gravity-only with kp = kd = 0; this is what you're in for the bulk of the calibration campaign. PD is the production law, used for verification once kg is dialed in.

Hold the limb before clicking TUNE. The node ramps from 0 to gravity-comp torque over 0.5 s, but if kg is already badly wrong, the ramped torque can still drive the joint to its limit. The GUI requires confirmation before activating any non-FREE mode for exactly this reason. The wrist yaw broke from a torque step that bypassed this kind of check.

The procedure — sim first, distal to proximal

The same launcher and the same node run in Gazebo and on real hardware. Sim is for catching the embarrassing failures (URDF chains that won't parse, controllers that won't spawn, a sign-flipped comp_sign that drives a joint into its limit) before they happen with current flowing through real motors. Real hardware is for the parts sim cannot tell you: the actual link masses, the actuator's static and Coulomb friction, the motor-mount mirrors that exist in the physical assembly but not in the URDF.

Calibrate from the wrist inward in both. Distal joints carry the smallest mass, so a wrong kg is least dangerous; their gravity term is also least entangled with upstream joints, so the kg you find at the wrist is closer to a clean reading of one link's mass error.

  • Pre-flight (no hardware) compute_initial_kg --chain left_arm prints the URDF-derived gravity torque at home, shoulder horizontal, elbow bent, and combined. Sanity-check magnitudes — for the left arm at horizontal extension you should see ~12 N·m at shoulder_roll. If numbers are an order of magnitude off, the URDF is broken before you touch hardware or sim.
  • Sim run ros2 launch humanoid_pd_tuning pd_tuning.launch.py use_sim:=true robot_group:=left_arm. Loads the full humanoid in Gazebo Harmonic with control_mode:=effort (URDF declares effort interfaces with X4/X6/X8 moderate-effort min/max — see actuator_properties.xacro). Same per-chain JointGroupEffortController as on real hardware; same tuning node; same GUI. Sim has perfect mass knowledge, so kg should converge to 1.000 — any deviation means the tuning loop itself is broken (wrong sign, wrong Kt conversion, controller not active). Fix it here.
  • Launch on real hardware with everything FREE ros2 launch humanoid_pd_tuning pd_tuning.launch.py use_sim:=false robot_group:=left_arm. The drives boot into CST via the existing ethercat_gravcomp/ configs. Every joint receives 0.0 effort. Support the arm by hand or in a jig — the limb will fall otherwise.
  • Tune one joint at a time Confirm the dialog, click TUNE on wrist_roll. The kg slider starts at 1.0. Move the joint by hand through its range and watch: if it drifts up, kg is too high; if it sags, too low. Adjust until the joint feels neutral across multiple poses. Click FREE, then TUNE the next joint.
  • Save "Save gains…" writes a YAML containing {name, motor, mode, kg, kp, kd, q_des, comp_sign, max_effort} per joint. Pass that file via gains_file:= on the next launch to start where you left off.
  • Tune kp, then kd Once every joint's kg is steady, "Capture current" then click PD. Raise kp until the joint resists displacement firmly under hand pressure; raise kd if it rings. The bench math from §05 (Kp = J ωn², Kd = 2ζ√(KpJ)) gives you the starting point — tune from there per joint.
  • Verify URDF mass verify_mass_from_gains gains/left_arm_tuned.yaml flags joints whose tuned kg deviates from 1 by more than 5%. A joint with kg = 1.15 indicates the URDF mass model is ~15% low for the links downstream of that joint. Edit full_robot_description/urdf/links/*.xacro, rebuild, retune. If the new kg lands at 1 ± 5%, the mass model is healthy.

Why upstream joints matter — the axis-tilt readout

The wrist roll axis is vertical when the shoulder and elbow are at home — gravity torque on that joint is identically zero, and no value of kg will move the arm. Lift the shoulder to horizontal and the wrist roll axis tilts toward horizontal too — now gravity has a lever arm and kg matters. PyKDL's JntToGravity already returns the torque at joint i taking the full upstream pose into account; the GUI also computes and displays the axis tilt directly so you understand why the gravity torque is what it is at the current pose.

Eq. 14b — axis tilt from gravity $$ \theta_{\text{tilt}}^{(i)} \;=\; \arccos\!\Big|\; \mathbf{R}_{0 \to i}(q)\,\hat a_i^{\text{local}} \cdot \hat g \;\Big| $$
R0→i(q)
rotation from chain root to joint i's parent frame, given current upstream angles q. Computed via ChainFkSolverPos_recursive.
âilocal
joint i's axis in its local frame, as defined in the URDF (e.g. (0,0,1) for a yaw joint).
ĝ
unit gravity vector in the root frame, (0,0,−1) for an upright torso.

The folded absolute value collapses the sign into [0°, 90°]. 0° means the axis is parallel to gravity — the joint sees no gravity torque from any downstream mass, and you can't tune kg in that pose. 90° means horizontal — full lever arm, maximum sensitivity. The GUI's tilt column tells you which poses to try when a joint's gravity reading looks suspiciously small.

The tilt at home column tells you whether the home pose actually exercises that joint's gravity term — for axis-aligned joints (wrist_roll, shoulder_yaw on the left arm at home), you have to deflect upstream joints first or the calibration is uninformative.

Worked example — first kg pass on the left arm

For the left arm at home, the URDF predicts shoulder_pitch τg ≈ −2.26 N·m with a chain mass of 5.11 kg. If during calibration that joint floats neutrally at kg = 1.08, the URDF is underestimating mass downstream of shoulder_pitch by ~8%. The offender is one of seven links (upper arm through hand_base). The biggest single contributor at home is the upper-arm assembly; bias the URDF correction there first, rebuild, retune, and watch kg on every distal joint converge toward 1 simultaneously.

Once both arms converge with kg within ±5% of unity and PD gains hold position firmly, recording.launch.py gets rewritten to spawn pd_tuning_node with the tuned gains_file and the legacy gravity_comp_node.py retires. From that point on, the same node that ran the calibration is what runs in production — and the gain numbers shipped to ONNX training (§12) are the same numbers running on the real arm by construction.
