Part I

The experiment.

A simple weight swap, done right, produces every number you need to characterize the actuator.

§ 01 — The three-payload experiment

Bar horizontal, swap weights, log everything.

You command the bar to horizontal and hold position via PD + gravity feedforward. You swap weights: nothing → 0.5 kg → 1 kg. The motor must produce three different steady-state torques to hold the same angle, and because you know the masses and the arm length, you know every torque exactly.

steady-state condition at horizontal $$ \tau_{ss} = m \cdot g \cdot l $$

With l = 0.30 m, the three operating points are:

Bar alone
τ_bar

Calibration baseline. Measure once, subtract everywhere else.

+ 0.5 kg payload
+1.47 N·m

Additional gravity torque beyond the bar's own contribution.

+ 1.0 kg payload
+2.94 N·m

Doubles cleanly from the 0.5 kg case — linearity check built in.

At steady state, velocity is zero so friction contributes nothing, acceleration is zero so inertia contributes nothing. The entire motor torque is gravity — which means every other unknown in the system drops out, and whatever the motor reports is directly interpretable.

§ 02 — What it actually teaches you

More than you'd expect, from three operating points.

About the actuator

  • Torque constant K_t, properly calibrated. Three known torques give you two independent K_t estimates and a linearity check. If they agree, your τ-vs-i relationship is clean. If they diverge, you've found current-loop saturation or nonlinearity before it bites you in a policy.
  • Gravity feedforward validation. If K_g holds perfectly at 1 kg but drifts at 0.5 kg, your feedforward isn't scaling with payload — it's hardcoded. On the humanoid, payload is unknown, so this must become a function, not a constant. More in Part III.
  • Real torque headroom. Datasheet peak/continuous ratings are generic. At your actual ambient temperature and your actual duty cycle, the safe continuous torque may be 20-30% below spec. Log thermal behavior during long holds.
  • Stiction contribution to steady-state error. Three payloads tell you whether the residual position error is constant (pure stiction) or scales with torque (bearing/gearbox load effect).
  • Cross-checked inertia. Small oscillations around horizontal at each payload let you measure closed-loop ω_n — and back out J_total vs payload.

About the EtherCAT path

  • Cycle jitter under real load. If 1 kg loads cause cycle-time variance the empty bar didn't, your master's scheduling breaks under current draw. This is the most common silent failure in robotics EtherCAT stacks.
  • Current-loop bandwidth, indirectly. Step commanded torque, watch actual torque via 6077h. Time to 63% = τ_m. Typically 2-10 ms.
  • PDO mapping completeness. The fact that you're running this experiment forces you to audit whether your PDO includes what you need. For the X6, this audit has a specific answer — see Part V.
  • Operation-mode latency. Total latency = EtherCAT transport + current loop. Subtract τ_m from the total and you have bus latency — the number that goes into sim as observation/action delay.
Every number from this experiment becomes a parameter in your sim actuator model or a sanity check on your ROS 2 stack. Nothing is academic.
Part II

Estimating the payload.

Three ways to infer mass from what the motor tells you — ground truth, sanity check, and the humanoid-grade online version.

§ 03 — Three methods, three information sources

Same physics, different sensors.

On your humanoid, the payload will be unknown. You need math that estimates it from available signals. Three methods, in order of complexity — each uses different information, each works when the others don't.

Method 1
Static torque balance
use first

Hold still at known angle. Read motor torque. Solve for payload. Simple, accurate, the ground-truth method for validating the others.

Method 2
Position-error inference
cross-check

Keep K_g fixed. Measure steady-state position error. Multiply by Kp to recover torque mismatch. Useful when torque readout is unreliable.

Method 3
Online RLS estimator
humanoid grade

Continuous estimation while the arm moves. Recursive least squares on a linear regression. Ports directly to the humanoid.

§ 04 — Method 1 — static torque balance

The direct approach.

Hold the bar horizontal. Zero velocity, zero acceleration. Friction contributes nothing, inertia contributes nothing, the entire motor torque is gravity. Rearrange for the payload:

Eq. 1 — payload from steady-state torque $$ \boxed{\; m_{payload} = \frac{\tau_{motor} - m_{bar} \cdot l_{bar,\text{COM}} \cdot g}{l_{payload} \cdot g} \;} $$
τ_motor
steady-state motor torque (from drive's torque feedback, converted to N·m — see Part V)
m_bar · l_bar,COM · g
bar-alone gravity torque; measured once as your baseline
l_payload
distance from axis to the weight's center of mass (0.30 m in your rig)

Concrete procedure

  • Calibrate the bar (no payload). Bar horizontal, PD active with working gains. Raise K_g slowly until steady-state position error is zero. Log the motor's reported torque (6077h, converted to N·m). Average over 5 seconds. Call this τ_bar.
  • Attach 0.5 kg. Hold horizontal for 5 seconds. Log motor torque. Average. Call this τ_loaded.
  • Solve. $$ m_{est} = \frac{\tau_{loaded} - \tau_{bar}}{l_{payload} \cdot g} = \frac{\tau_{loaded} - \tau_{bar}}{0.30 \times 9.81} $$ Expected result: ≈ 0.5 kg ± 5%.
  • Repeat with 1 kg. Expected result: ≈ 1.0 kg. If the two estimates scale linearly with the known masses, your system is well-calibrated. If they diverge, something (K_t, l_payload, encoder zero) is off.

±5% on mass estimate, mostly limited by K_t uncertainty. On the X6 specifically, K_t is derived from rated-torque / rated-current since it isn't published — so your K_t error bar is tied to the rated-current value you configured, not an independently measured property. Budget for this.

§ 05 — Method 2 — position error inference

When you don't trust the torque readout.

If your drive's torque feedback is miscalibrated or unavailable, you can still infer gravity torque from position error. When K_g is wrong, the PD loop makes up the difference — and that appears as steady-state θ_error.

Eq. 2 — steady-state torque balance with wrong K_g $$ K_g \sin\theta + K_p(\theta_d - \theta_{actual}) = m_{total} \cdot l \cdot g \cdot \sin\theta $$

Setting θ_d = horizontal (sin θ = 1) and rearranging:

Eq. 3 — position error from K_g mismatch $$ \boxed{\; \theta_{error} = \frac{m_{total} \cdot l \cdot g - K_g}{K_p} \;} $$

Concrete example

You have K_g = 3.0 (calibrated for 1 kg), swap to 0.5 kg. True gravity torque drops from 2.94 to 1.47 N·m. K_g is now 1.53 N·m too high.

Kp valuePredicted θ_errorPhysical meaning
3.0 (soft)−0.51 rad≈ −29° — bar rises well above horizontal before PD pulls it back
22.95 (target)−0.067 rad≈ −3.8° — small visible error, still identifiable by the encoder
100 (stiff)−0.015 rad≈ −0.9° — near noise floor, hard to measure cleanly
Soft gains amplify the signal you're trying to measure. If you want to identify K_g mismatch via position error, lower Kp, don't raise it — counter to the usual "stiff is better" intuition.
§ 06 — Method 3 — online RLS estimator

Estimating payload while moving.

Methods 1 and 2 require you to stop. Method 3 estimates payload continuously as the arm moves — which is what the humanoid will actually need. The trick: the full rigid-body equation is linear in m_p, so it becomes a scalar linear regression.

Setting up the regression

Eq. 4 — full joint dynamics, parameterized by m_p $$ \tau_{motor} = (J_{bar} + m_p l_p^2)\ddot\theta + b\dot\theta + \tau_f + (m_{bar,eff} + m_p l_p) g \sin\theta $$

Group known quantities on the left, m_p coefficient on the right:

Eq. 5 — rearranged as y = φ · m_p $$ \underbrace{\tau_{motor} - J_{bar}\ddot\theta - b\dot\theta - \tau_f - m_{bar,eff} g \sin\theta}_{y} = \underbrace{(l_p^2 \ddot\theta + l_p g \sin\theta)}_{\phi} \cdot m_p $$

At every control tick you get a fresh (y, φ) pair. Recursive least squares converges m_p to its true value within a few seconds of motion.

Recursive least squares update

Eq. 6 — RLS with forgetting factor $$ P[k+1] = \frac{P[k]}{\lambda + \phi^2 P[k]} $$ $$ \hat{m}_p[k+1] = \hat{m}_p[k] + P[k+1] \cdot \phi \cdot (y - \phi \cdot \hat{m}_p[k]) $$
P
estimate covariance (initialize large, ~1e3)
λ
forgetting factor, 0.99 — lets the estimate adapt if payload changes
φ
regressor value at this tick
y
"known" side of the equation at this tick
Persistence of excitation. If the bar sits still, φ is nearly constant and the estimate won't converge. You need motion. On the bench, command a slow sinusoid (±15° at 0.3 Hz) — the estimate will lock in within a few seconds.

Minimal Python

import numpy as np

# Known quantities from your bench identification
J_bar = 0.012       # kg·m², bar alone
m_bar_eff = 0.15    # bar's effective gravity contribution coefficient
l_p = 0.30          # payload offset, m
b = 0.05            # viscous friction
Fc = 0.15           # Coulomb friction
g = 9.81

# RLS state
m_hat = 0.0
P = 1e3
lam = 0.99

def rls_update(tau, theta, theta_dot, theta_ddot):
    global m_hat, P

    # Torque explained by everything except the payload
    tau_known = (J_bar * theta_ddot
                 + b * theta_dot
                 + Fc * np.tanh(theta_dot / 0.01)
                 + m_bar_eff * g * np.sin(theta))
    y = tau - tau_known

    # Regressor — everything that m_p multiplies
    phi = l_p**2 * theta_ddot + l_p * g * np.sin(theta)

    # RLS update
    P = P / (lam + phi**2 * P)
    m_hat = m_hat + P * phi * (y - phi * m_hat)
    return m_hat

Call rls_update at every control tick with the current state and measured torque. After a few seconds of motion, m_hat sits near the true payload.

Part III

Adaptive gravity.

The K_g term hardcoded for 1 kg breaks the moment the payload changes. Here's how to make it adapt without breaking the loop.

§ 07 — Why Kp = Kd = 0 doesn't work

Pure feedforward is unstable.

The intuitive idea — "turn off PD, let the motor produce K_g·sin(θ), if K_g is right the bar balances anywhere" — is mathematically correct and operationally doomed. Here's why it fails.

With Kp = Kd = 0, the motor torque is $$ \tau_{motor} = K_g \sin\theta $$

If K_g exactly equals mgl, the motor perfectly cancels gravity and the bar hangs in neutral equilibrium. In theory.

In practice:

  • Stiction hides small disturbances until they accumulate past the break-away threshold, then the bar breaks loose suddenly.
  • Any K_g error drives acceleration. Wrong by 0.1 N·m at J = 0.1 kg·m² → 1 rad/s² — within a second the bar is already at ±0.5 rad and accelerating.
  • Sensor noise in θ propagates through sin(θ) and becomes torque noise with no damping to attenuate it.
  • No damping means no dissipation. Energy put into the system by disturbances has nowhere to go.
The general principle. Feedforward compensates for known dynamics. PD compensates for unknown disturbances and model error. Remove PD and you're betting your feedforward is perfect AND the world has no disturbances — neither is true in practice.
§ 08 — The right architecture: PD + adaptive K_g

PD provides the signal; K_g absorbs the correction.

Keep PD active with working gains. Add an online K_g estimator that watches the PD torque contribution — because if PD is working to hold position, it's exactly telling you how wrong K_g is.

Eq. 7 — control law with adaptive K_g $$ \tau_{motor} = \hat{K}_g \sin\theta + K_p(\theta_d - \theta) + K_d(\dot\theta_d - \dot\theta) $$

In steady state at the commanded angle, the PD contribution should be zero — any residual is pure K_g mismatch. So the update rule is:

Eq. 8 — K_g estimator update $$ \boxed{\; \hat{K}_g[k+1] = \hat{K}_g[k] + \eta \cdot \sin\theta \cdot \tau_{PD}[k] \;} $$
τ_PD
the PD portion of the motor torque: K_p · e + K_d · ė
η
learning rate; start small (~0.1) and increase if convergence is sluggish
sin θ
projects the PD error into the gravity axis — matters when the bar is far from horizontal

Why this works

When you swap from 1 kg to 0.5 kg, the sequence is:

  1. Instantaneously K_g = 2.94 is too high; motor over-commands by 1.47 N·m.
  2. Bar rises above horizontal.
  3. PD produces negative τ_PD to pull it back.
  4. Negative τ_PD feeds the update rule, decrementing K_g.
  5. Over hundreds of milliseconds, K_g decays from 2.94 toward 1.47.
  6. PD error returns to near-zero. K_g settles at the new correct value.

Pick η so the estimator's settling time is 5–10× the PD's settling time. The PD is the fast loop keeping the bar near target while K_g slowly updates underneath. Too fast: estimator fights the PD and oscillates. Too slow: you see large transient errors when payload changes.

Humanoid relevance

This structure — estimator layer, control layer, policy layer — is how mature humanoid stacks work. A parameter estimator continuously updates mass/inertia properties; a control layer uses those estimates for feedforward; a policy provides high-level behavior. You're discovering the architecture from first principles on one joint. That's a good sign.

The interesting humanoid question becomes: how fast does K_g need to track? If the arm picks up a 2 kg object in 100 ms, does your estimator catch up in 100 ms or 2 seconds? That determines whether the robot stumbles or handles it gracefully. Your pendulum gives you a direct measurement of this number.

Continuous rotation enables clean identification

Your pendulum can spin past vertical — you're not limited to small-angle linearization. For the K_g estimator specifically, this means a slow full rotation excites sin(θ) across its entire range: +1, 0, −1, 0, +1. Best possible persistence of excitation. The estimate locks on within one or two rotations, vs many seconds of wiggling near horizontal.

Part IV

The full protocol.

Seven identification tests, sequenced so each sets up the next — one afternoon of work, a complete actuator profile at the end.

§ 09 — The continuous-rotation advantage

A pendulum that can spin gives you more signal.

Most motor ID protocols assume limited joint travel. Yours doesn't — the bar can spin past vertical freely. Three experimental upgrades this unlocks:

  • Faster estimator convergence. Slow sweeps through ±180° visit every sin(θ) value. The K_g and payload estimators lock in within one or two rotations.
  • More information per test. Instead of three static measurements, you get a full τ(θ) curve per payload. Amplitude is mgl; phase tells you where true horizontal is; asymmetry flags constant bias torques (loose mounting, cable drag).
  • Clean separation of gravity and friction. Drive at constant θ̇ through a full rotation. Gravity averages to zero over a cycle (∫sin dθ = 0 over 2π); friction has a constant sign per half-rotation. Plot τ vs θ — mean is friction, oscillation amplitude is gravity. Two numbers from one test.
Encoder wrap. Multiturn encoders handle continuous rotation cleanly. Single-turn incremental encoders wrap at 2π and produce fake discontinuities that destroy θ̇ and θ̈ calculations. Most EtherCAT servos expose both "actual position" (unwrapped multiturn) and "position within revolution" — use the multiturn signal. The X6 has dual absolute encoders; verify which one 6064h is reporting before you trust θ̈ at zero crossings.
§ 10 — The seven identification tests

What to measure, how to measure it.

10.1 · EtherCAT cycle jitter

Log PDO inter-arrival timestamps for 10 seconds. Mean at 1 kHz should be 1000 μs; standard deviation under 20 μs (2%) is healthy. Past 100 μs is scheduling trouble — fix before trusting any other measurement, because jitter contaminates θ̇ and θ̈ calculations.

10.2 · Torque command → shaft torque (K_t verification)

This is the most important test, because if it's wrong, every other number is wrong too.

In CST mode, the chain from command to shaft torque is:

$$ \text{cmd (‰)} \;\to\; \text{drive} \; (\times \, I_{rated} / 1000) \;\to\; \text{current loop} \;\to\; K_t \times i \;\to\; \text{shaft torque} $$

Three places reality can diverge from specification:

  • The rated current parameter configured in the drive (firmware default 10 A on the X6; nameplate 9.5 Arms). If this is wrong, every ‰ command scales wrong.
  • The K_t itself — datasheet nominal drifts ±5-10% per unit due to magnet tolerances and temperature.
  • Current-loop error (usually <1% but worth confirming).

Procedure. Static hold, horizontal, 1 kg payload. You know true gravity torque is 2.943 N·m. Log three things:

  • Commanded 6071h (in ‰ of rated current).
  • Actual 6077h (torque/current feedback, also in ‰ of rated current on the X6).
  • If you have a current sensor or the CAN interface available, actual phase current in amperes.

Convert commanded ‰ to expected N·m using the drive's rated-current and your K_t estimate. Compare to 2.943. Systematic offset → K_t or I_rated wrong. Verify by running the same test at 0.5 kg and 0 kg — the offset should scale linearly with load.

10.3 · Inertia cross-check

Your analytical J ≈ 0.102 kg·m² assumed a thin uniform bar. Cross-check:

  • Pendulum method: remove the weight, let the bar swing freely, measure the period T over 10+ cycles. $J = m_{bar} g l_{bar,COM} T^2 / (4\pi^2)$.
  • Torque-step method: at horizontal (known gravity torque), command a torque step, measure angular acceleration over the first 10–20 ms. $J = (\tau_{cmd} - \tau_{gravity}) / \ddot\theta$.

Both methods should agree within 10%. Divergence means the bar mass distribution differs from your assumption, or rotor/reducer inertia reflected at the output is larger than you modeled.

10.4 · Friction identification

Command constant velocities through continuous rotation: ±0.5, ±1.0, ±1.5, ±2.0 rad/s. At steady velocity, gravity averages to zero over a rotation and acceleration is zero, so:

$$ \tau_{motor} = F_c \cdot \text{sign}(\dot\theta) + F_v \cdot \dot\theta $$

Plot τ vs θ̇ — slope is F_v, y-intercept is ±F_c. Record F_c⁺ and F_c⁻ separately; gearbox friction is often asymmetric.

Stiction: at rest, ramp torque slowly upward, note the torque at which motion begins. Typically 1.5–2× F_c.

10.5 · Current-loop bandwidth (τ_m)

Command a torque step (0 → 3 N·m). Log actual torque feedback at 1 kHz. Time to 63% of final value is τ_m. Expected 2–10 ms for a well-tuned servo.

If τ_m is much slower than specification, current-loop gains are conservative or a filter you didn't configure is active. If τ_m changes between payloads, current loop is saturating — a bad sign.

10.6 · Backlash

Command slow triangular position profile ±10°, 10-second period. Log commanded vs measured. Direction-reversal points show backlash as a flat region where measured position stays still while commanded changes. Measure the flat width in radians.

On a harmonic or planetary reducer like the X6's 19.6:1, backlash should be arc-minutes. If it's degrees, something is wrong mechanically.

10.7 · Dynamic K_g / payload estimator validation

Run the full adaptive-K_g experiment from §08 with all ground truth already established: Kp and Kd known, θ_d = horizontal, swap payloads, log K_g(t). Compare measured convergence against the 5–10× PD settling time target. Log the final K_g values at each payload and verify they match the mgl prediction.

Then run Method 3 online RLS on the same data, verify m_hat converges to the known payload within a few seconds. You've now validated both estimators against ground truth on real hardware — they can go to the humanoid with confidence.

§ 11 — Running order

Seven tests, one afternoon.

Sequencing matters. Each test's result becomes an input to later tests. Run in this order:

Step 01 · ~10 min
EtherCAT cycle jitter

Before trusting any measurement, confirm the bus is healthy. Log PDO timestamps, compute jitter statistics. If unhealthy, fix scheduling before continuing — otherwise everything downstream is noisy for the wrong reasons.

Output: cycle jitter σ < 20 μs ideally
Step 02 · ~15 min
K_t / torque command check

Static holds at three payloads. Establishes that commanded ‰ actually produces expected N·m. Without this, every subsequent torque-based measurement is biased.

Output: calibrated K_t or identified rated-current offset
Step 03 · ~15 min
Inertia (pendulum swing + torque step)

Pendulum swing for passive J estimate; torque step for active J estimate. Two independent methods → cross-check ≤ 10%.

Output: J measured, ± variance
Step 04 · ~20 min
Friction (constant-velocity sweeps + stiction ramp)

Continuous-rotation sweeps at 4–8 velocities in both directions. Linear fit → F_v. Extrapolated y-intercept → F_c. Slow torque ramp at rest → F_s.

Output: F_v, F_c⁺, F_c⁻, F_s
Step 05 · ~10 min
Current-loop τ_m (torque step)

Step commanded torque from zero to a moderate value while holding against a reference position. Time to 63% of actual torque final value = τ_m. Repeat at two amplitudes to detect saturation.

Output: τ_m, linearity flag
Step 06 · ~10 min
Backlash (slow triangular profile)

Quick check only for a harmonic-reducer actuator. Triangular position ±10°, look at direction reversal dead zones.

Output: backlash in radians (likely arc-minutes)
Step 07 · ~20 min
Dynamic K_g + payload estimator validation

All ground truth in hand — run adaptive K_g through the payload-swap sequence. Run Method 3 RLS on the same data. Both estimators should converge to known values.

Output: validated estimators, convergence time, ready for humanoid

Total: about 100 minutes of experiments plus an hour of post-processing. At the end you have a complete single-joint parameter profile and two validated online estimators.

Part V

The X6 reality.

What MyActuator's V4 EtherCAT protocol actually exposes — and what it doesn't. Based on Protocol V1.0, April 2025 revision.

§ 12 — What the drive exposes

One current signal, per-mille scaled, no temperature.

Your suspicion was correct. The V4 X-series EtherCAT interface does not implement CiA 402 object 6078h "Current actual value." The only current/torque feedback is 6077h "Torque actual value" — and on the X6, its unit is per-mille of rated current, not rated torque. This is a deviation from how most CiA 402 drives use the same object, and it shapes your entire identification workflow.

6077h on the X6 reports q-axis current scaled to ‰ of rated current (nameplate 9.5 Arms, firmware default 10 A). To get amperes: multiply by I_rated / 1000. To get torque: multiply amperes by K_t. And K_t is not published — you derive it from rated_torque / rated_current. So your torque reading has two stacked calibration dependencies, and the I_rated value is configured over CAN, not EtherCAT.

  • I_rated = 13.40 A — read via MyActuator setup tool over CAN; neither the firmware default 10 A nor the nameplate 9.5 A applied.
  • K_t spec = 2.1 N·m/A; effective K_t = 2.0095 N·m/A (4.5% under spec — gearbox/temperature/magnet tolerance), derived from a static-hold calibration per §10.2.
  • Rated output torque = K_t · I_rated = 26.93 N·m. The EtherCAT command factor must be calibrated against this product, not against any nominal "rated torque" number.
  • EtherCAT command factor = 1000 / 26.93 = 37.14 (state factor 0.02693). Was 50.0 (assumed 20 N·m rated) — produced 41% over-amplification at the shaft.

Deriving the EtherCAT YAML factor

The forward chain from §10.2 is:

$$ T_{shaft} \;=\; T_{cmd} \cdot \frac{\text{factor}}{1000} \cdot I_{rated} \cdot K_t $$

The factor is the EtherCAT YAML scaling on the command interface — the factor: field on the 6071h command_interface: effort entry in config/ethercat/icub_x6_drive.yaml. For a 1:1 mapping where commanded N·m equals shaft N·m (so the policy can ignore per-mille entirely), set:

Eq. — command factor (N·m → ‰) $$ \text{factor}_{cmd} \;=\; \frac{1000}{K_t \cdot I_{rated}} $$

The state-interface factor (‰ → N·m on the feedback path, 6077h) is the inverse:

Eq. — state factor (‰ → N·m) $$ \text{factor}_{state} \;=\; \frac{K_t \cdot I_{rated}}{1000} $$

The product K_t · I_rated is the rated output torque — the physical quantity that 1000‰ corresponds to. Both factors break the moment either input changes: a firmware reflash that resets I_rated, a motor swap with a different K_t (or even the same model with magnet-tolerance drift), or any drive parameter touched via the MyActuator CAN tool. There is no automatic check from the EtherCAT side because the drive only reports per-mille; the absolute scale is whatever the master assumes.

What this means for your ID protocol

  • Before any torque-based test, pin down I_rated via MyActuator's Setup Software over CAN, and log the exact value.
  • K_t should be measured, not trusted. Your three-payload experiment is exactly the right test for this — it gives you two independent K_t estimates from gravity-torque ground truth.
  • No temperature telemetry over EtherCAT. If thermal drift matters for your identification, add an external thermistor or monitor via CAN.
  • No DC bus voltage. If regenerative braking during high-torque transients matters, instrument the supply separately.
  • No error code object (603Fh). Diagnostics must come from the status word (6041h) and out-of-band CAN tool.
Scale convention deviation. On most CiA 402 drives (Synapticon, Kollmorgen, Inovance IS620N), 6077h reports ‰ of rated torque — so cmd=1000 means rated torque. On the MyActuator V4, 6077h reports ‰ of rated current. If you copy control code from another drive, your torque numbers will be wrong by a factor of your gearbox efficiency times K_t. Always verify scaling on a new drive family.

§ 14 — Seven implementation gotchas

Bugs you'll hit if you don't know.

  • 6077h is per-mille of rated CURRENT, not rated torque Deviation from how most CiA 402 drives behave. If you copy torque-handling code from another vendor's driver, the conversion will be wrong by gearbox_efficiency × K_t.
  • I_rated defaults to 10 A but nameplate is 9.5 Arms — and the actual setting can differ by tens of percent Pin this down via MyActuator's CAN-based setup tool before any torque scaling work. Every ‰-to-ampere conversion depends on it. Default vs nameplate is a ~5% bias; on the pendulum_test rig as of 2026-04-28 it was 13.40 A (40% above firmware default), which made the EtherCAT YAML's factor: 50.0 deliver 1.41× the commanded torque until corrected to 37.14.
  • No torque slope (6087h) Writes to 6071h take effect on the very next cycle. For humanoid impact/landing scenarios, implement a master-side rate limiter so you don't command 0 → 60 N·m in one tick.
  • Regen on a 48 V bus During CST deceleration against inertia, regenerative current raises the bus voltage. Manual recommends a bleeder resistor or battery for sustained regen. On bench with a lab supply, watch for overvoltage protection trips.
  • PDF typos that bite The CST walkthrough in the official manual says "Set the target speed 0x607" (should be 0x6071). Status-word bit 12 description is copy-pasted between CSV and CST sections with inconsistent meaning. Cross-reference with the ESI file and your own SDO probes.
  • Default PDO mapping not published in the PDF The 1C12h/1C13h assignments and 1600h/1A00h listings are only in the ESI XML. Download MT-Device-250702.XML and parse it — don't assume.
  • DC-Synchron mode is required The ESC is ET1100-uC16. Manual instructs configuring DC-Synchron in TwinCAT. Free-run mode will appear to work but drift under load. Always use DC-Synchron on this drive.
§ 15 — The dual-interface workflow

CAN for setup, EtherCAT for runtime.

Because the motor characterization parameters are not exposed over EtherCAT, commission the drive over CAN and use EtherCAT for your real-time control loop. Both interfaces are physically present on the X6.

Commissioning phase (CAN, one-time per drive)

  • Connect via MyActuator's Windows Setup Software V4.0.
  • Set and verify rated current (I_rated). Log the exact value.
  • Configure current-loop PI gains if defaults aren't adequate.
  • Set protection limits: max current, max speed, max temperature.
  • Read back and log every configured parameter — they cannot be read over EtherCAT later.
  • Optionally: request dyno parameters from Arics Tang (tangxc@myactuator.com) for unpublished K_t/inertia data per unit.

Runtime phase (EtherCAT, every control tick)

  • CST mode, 1 kHz cyclic. Command 6071h, read 6077h, 6064h, 6041h.
  • Convert 6077h ‰ → amperes (multiply by logged I_rated / 1000) → N·m (multiply by K_t, accounting for gearbox).
  • Monitor 6041h for drive state transitions; fail to a safe mode on unexpected state.
  • Log all received PDOs at 1 kHz to ros2 bag for post-hoc analysis.

Diagnostics phase (optional, CAN)

For motor temperature, DC bus voltage, or detailed error codes, switch the motor to CAN briefly. Or run a dedicated diagnostic loop that queries via CAN every few seconds without interrupting the EtherCAT control loop. The interfaces can coexist.

The pragmatic truth: MyActuator's V4 EtherCAT exposes the minimum CiA 402 subset needed for real-time control, nothing more. Every other useful parameter lives on the CAN side. If distinct amperes feedback is a hard requirement for your humanoid design, raise it with MyActuator before hardware-lock — it's a real capability gap they could address in firmware.
